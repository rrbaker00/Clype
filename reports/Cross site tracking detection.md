# Prove the link, not just the tracker's presence

Clype can move from "tracker X appears on several audited sites" to "tracker X got the same ID for this browser on site A and site B" with plain Python over data it already records, plus one extra browsing pass. The most reliable method in the research literature is to find **identifier values** (stay the same within a run, differ between two fresh browser profiles, 8–100 characters), then look for those values in **requests to a different registrable domain**: in the URL, Referer, redirect `Location`, Cookie header or POST body, raw or encoded. That one matcher detects cookie syncing, first-party cookie exfiltration (`_ga`, `_fbp`), CNAME-cloaked leaks and UID smuggling. Clype's current cross-audit view cannot prove linkage, because every audit uses a fresh, isolated context, so a tracker gets a *different* ID on every site by design. Proof needs a dedicated **same-context linkage pass**. That pass is valid in 2026: Chrome never removed third-party cookies, and Playwright's bundled Chromium 156 was measured sending an unpartitioned `SameSite=None; Secure` cookie set on site A back to the same third party on site B. The legal stakes are clear. The amended PECR reg. 6 and Schedule A1 (in force 5 Feb 2026) add a statistics exemption, but ICO guidance says **"You must get consent for any cross-site or cross-device tracking"** and that advertising "never" was exempt. Every one of these findings observed after "Reject all" is therefore a strong complaint point, as long as Clype words it as what was *sent*, not what the recipient *did*. The ranked plan at the end gives five techniques, in build order, with rules, thresholds and finding wording.

## Fresh contexts make cross-audit presence useless as proof of linking

Linking evidence depends first on how the browser behaves, and Playwright differs from real Chrome in ways that matter. Google dropped forced third-party-cookie deprecation in July 2024 ([MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2024/07/22/google-keeping-third-party-cookies-chrome/)). In April 2025 it confirmed it would keep "third-party cookie choice" with no new prompt ([Privacy Sandbox blog](https://privacysandbox.google.com/blog/privacy-sandbox-next-steps)). On 17 Oct 2025 it retired most Privacy Sandbox APIs, including **Related Website Sets**, while keeping **CHIPS** and FedCM ([Privacy Sandbox blog](https://privacysandbox.google.com/blog/update-on-plans-for-privacy-sandbox-technologies)). So the right real-user baseline in 2026 is that **normal Chrome allows third-party cookies by default** and Incognito blocks them. A cross-site cookie Clype sees being sent really does reach default-Chrome users.

The research session tested Playwright Python 1.64.1 with Chromium 156.0.8078.4 headless on a local test harness: one context, two top-level sites, one embedded third party. The **unpartitioned `SameSite=None; Secure` cookie set on site A was sent to the third party on site B**. A `Partitioned` (CHIPS) cookie was stored twice, each with its own `partitionKey`, and never crossed sites. A cookie without a SameSite attribute was rejected in the third-party iframe because the default is Lax. `browser.new_context().cookies()` returned `[]`, which confirms contexts are fully isolated. That last point is why the current "same company on many audited sites" view is **presence, not proof**: in separate contexts the tracker cannot recognise the browser, and its cookie values will differ on every site.

There is a trap. Playwright launches Chromium with `--disable-features=...ThirdPartyStoragePartitioning...` to fix a test regression ([playwright#32230](https://github.com/microsoft/playwright/issues/32230)). Real Chrome has partitioned third-party localStorage, IndexedDB and similar storage since Chrome 115 ([Privacy Sandbox: storage partitioning](https://privacysandbox.google.com/3pcd/storage-partitioning)). In the local test, iframe localStorage was **identical across both top-level sites**, and attempts to turn partitioning back on were inconclusive. **Never claim cross-site recognition from third-party localStorage seen in Playwright.** It is a Playwright artefact. Claim it only from unpartitioned cookies, or from IDs carried in URLs or bodies. Playwright returns `partitionKey` on partitioned cookies only (the field was added in [v1.54](https://github.com/microsoft/playwright/releases/tag/v1.54.0)). That gives a simple rule: a cookie is **cross-site capable** if its domain is a third party, `sameSite == "None"`, `secure` is true, and `"partitionKey" not in cookie`. The `--test-third-party-cookie-phaseout` flag ([Chromium](https://www.chromium.org/Home/chromium-privacy/privacy-sandbox/third-party-cookie-phaseout)) did not block cookies in the localhost/IP test. That result is **unverified on real HTTPS domains**, so don't rely on it for a "3PC-blocked" comparison run.

## Twelve years of research use one ID test: constant within a run, different between runs

From Acar et al. 2014 to the 2021–2023 first-party-cookie studies, every paper uses the same differential test. Acar's rules: drop cookies expiring within a month, split values on delimiters, drop fields that change within a crawl, and compare the remaining fields across "two unrelated crawls on different machines". Fields that differ in length, or are more than 33% similar by Ratcliff-Obershelp, are dropped. The authors call the method "strict and conservative" with "a very low false positive rate" ([Acar et al., CCS'14](https://securehomes.esat.kuleuven.be/~gacar/persistent/the_web_never_forgets.pdf)). OpenWPM's version is the one most reused. Parse values as `(name=)value` pieces split on any character outside `a-zA-Z0-9 -=`. A piece is an ID if the cookie **expires more than 90 days out**, **8 ≤ length ≤ 100**, the value stays **constant throughout the measurement**, and it **differs between machines with similarity < 66%** ([Englehardt & Narayanan, CCS'16](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~arvindn/publications/OpenWPM_1_million_site_tracking_measurement.pdf)). Chen et al. added a fix that matters for compound values such as `GA1.1.<rand>.<ts>`. Strip timestamps, then repeatedly remove common subsequences longer than 2 characters, then score what is left at the 66% cutoff ([Chen et al., WWW'21](https://spt.cut.ac.cy/papers/chen_www21.pdf)). Fouad et al. drop the lifetime rule entirely, because "domains can continuously update cookies with a short lifetime and do the mapping … on the server side". They treat values identical in both crawls as "safe" ([Fouad et al., PETS'20](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01514)). The UID-smuggling study reached the same conclusion: a 90-day filter would have dropped **16% of real UIDs** ([Randall et al., IMC'22](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.10188)).

The cheap version fits Clype well. Chen et al. already ran two separate Chromium instances "immediately after the other" rather than on two machines ([Chen et al.](https://spt.cut.ac.cy/papers/chen_www21.pdf)). For Clype, that means the main audit context A, plus a second fresh context B that loads only the landing page right after A. Python's `difflib.SequenceMatcher(None, a, b).ratio()` implements Ratcliff-Obershelp. Two caveats are honest gaps. No paper measures the error that running on one machine and one IP adds. And none of the rule-based heuristics has published precision or recall: they were validated by manual inspection only. The ML classifier CookieGraph reports 90.07% precision and 92.09% recall, but for a different task (classifying first-party tracking cookies) ([Munir et al., CCS'23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.12370)). **No source gives a validated Shannon-entropy threshold.** Entropy should only be a tiebreak when context B never saw the cookie, and those results should be labelled "likely ID (unverified)".

## Each linking mechanism leaves a different trace in request data

**Cookie syncing** gives the strongest single-site evidence that two firms can join their views of one browser. OpenWPM counts two parties as synced when "a cookie ID appears in specific locations within the referrer, request, and location URLs". It also gives direction rules. In the request URL, the requested domain receives. In the Referer, the referring domain sends. In `Location`, the original domain sends and the redirect target receives. The authors warn that this captures "both intentional and accidental ID sharing" and is "an upper bound" ([OpenWPM](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~arvindn/publications/OpenWPM_1_million_site_tracking_measurement.pdf)). The scale is large. doubleclick.net shared **108 cookies with 118 third parties**, and 45 of the top 50 third parties synced (same source). In real users' traffic, the median synced ID reached **3.5 domains**, and syncing multiplied the number of domains tracking a user **6.75×** ([Papadopoulos et al., WWW'19](https://arxiv.org/html/1805.10505v3)). Their CONRAD method also searches URL *paths*, and removes same-owner pairs such as amazon.com and amazonaws.com. Google documents its exact match URLs: `cm.g.doubleclick.net/pixel?google_nid=…&google_cm`, redirects that carry `google_gid=<web-safe base64>`, `google_push`, `google_hm`, and `google_error=3` when the user has no Google cookie ([Google Authorized Buyers cookie guide](https://developers.google.com/authorized-buyers/rtb/cookie-guide)). Prebid's docs say user syncs should not fire "when cookies are disabled or denied". Syncs after "Reject all" therefore show that the CMP-to-Prebid consent wiring failed ([Prebid setConfig](https://docs.prebid.org/dev-docs/publisher-api-reference/setConfig.html)). Prebid's userId module sends cross-site identity-graph IDs (ID5, UID2, LiveRamp, SharedID, Criteo and others) as `user.ext.eids[].source/uids[].id` in bid requests ([Prebid userId](https://docs.prebid.org/dev-docs/modules/userId.html)).

**First-party cookie exfiltration** is very common, but on its own it is a per-site ID. Chen et al. found that **57.66%** of sites with external cookies send at least one unique ID to multiple third parties ([Chen et al.](https://spt.cut.ac.cy/papers/chen_www21.pdf)). CookieGraph found **96.61%** of first-party tracking cookies are "ghostwritten" by third-party scripts ([Munir et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.12370)). Matching needs format knowledge. GA's cookie is `GAX.Y.Z.C`, but the value sent is only `Z.C` ([Fouad et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01514)). GA4 sends it as `cid`, and `_p` is a per-request cache-buster. That mapping comes from a secondary source, [martech.zone](https://martech.zone/google-analytics-image-pixel-variables/), and is unverified against Google docs. Meta's `_fbp` is `fb.1.<ms>.<random>`, sent as `fbp` ([Meta docs](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/conversions-api/parameters/fbp-and-fbc/)). A `_ga` value differs on every site. So it becomes **cross-site** evidence only when the same request also carries an unpartitioned third-party cookie or a synced ID that the vendor can join on, or when the vendor receives the identical token from two top-level sites in one context.

**CNAME cloaking** hides a tracker behind a first-party subdomain. Dimova et al. found it on **9.98% of the top 10,000 sites**. On **95%** of those sites, cookies set by other parties leaked to the cloaked host, and on 10 sites authentication cookies leaked. Their exclusions were cookies set by the tracker itself, session cookies, short cookies, and cookies seen on many sites. They warn that DNS data alone "leads to an overestimation" ([Dimova et al., PETS'21](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.09301)). The prevalence figures come from **October 2020** data, and no 2024–2026 figures were found. Dao et al. classed a chain as tracking "if any node in a chain was flagged" against EasyPrivacy or AdGuard ([APNIC blog](https://blog.apnic.net/2020/08/04/characterizing-cname-cloaking-based-tracking/)). AdGuard maintains `combined_original_trackers.txt` under the MIT licence ([AdGuard cname-trackers](https://github.com/AdguardTeam/cname-trackers)). On its own, CNAME cloaking is mostly per-site linking. Criteo deployed it only to Safari users, so Chromium can miss it (Dimova).

**Link decoration and UID smuggling** carry an ID from site X into site Y's URL and get around partitioned storage entirely. Randall et al. found smuggling on **about 8% of navigations** and bounce tracking with no UID in the URL on **2.7%**. Top redirectors included adclick.g.doubleclick.net (11.2%) and gm.demdex.net. **41% of dedicated smugglers were missing from the Disconnect list** ([Randall et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.10188)). Chrome's bounce-tracking mitigation runs only for users who block third-party cookies ([Privacy Sandbox](https://privacysandbox.google.com/protections/bounce-tracking-mitigations)). Default Chrome therefore offers no protection against it. Server-side syncing and bid-stream sharing are invisible to any browser. Bashir et al. found flows "where existing heuristic methods fail" ([Bashir et al., USENIX Sec'16](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.00920)). Every Clype report should state this limitation.

## False positives fall into five predictable groups

**Nonces and timestamps** are removed by the within-run constancy test. Any (receiver, parameter) whose value changes between two requests in one context is not an ID; GA's `_p` is one example. **Population-wide constants** are removed by the cross-context diff. These include property IDs (`G-`, `UA-`, `GTM-`, pixel IDs), locale codes, A/B buckets, version strings and hashes of page URLs. Privacy Badger takes the same approach from the other side: any third-party cookie value *not* on a short low-entropy allowlist counts as tracking. The allowlist covers yes/no/true/false (3 bits), single digits (4 bits) and language codes (8 bits), with a 12-bit total cap. For first-party values leaking to third-party image URLs, it requires a shared substring of at least 8 characters with estimated entropy above 33 bits ([Privacy Badger heuristicblocking.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/EFForg/privacybadger/master/src/js/heuristicblocking.js)). **Consent strings** (`euconsent-v2`, `gdpr_consent`, `OptanonConsent`, `CookieConsent`, GA `gcs`/`gcd`) are long but shared by everyone with the same choices. Exclude them from ID matching and keep them as evidence of the consent signal. OneTrust's embedded `consentId` and Cookiebot's `stamp` can be unique per user, but they are consent-record IDs, not tracking. That exclusion list is **engineering judgement**: no paper publishes one. **Same-owner flows** (google-analytics ↔ doubleclick, facebook ↔ fbcdn) need an entity map such as Tracker Radar's `owner` ([Tracker Radar data model](https://github.com/duckduckgo/tracker-radar/blob/main/docs/DATA_MODEL.md)) or CONRAD's same-owner filter. **Accidental sharing** is real leakage but not syncing: a site's own session ID in its query string, which then leaks via Referer. Label it "ID leaked via Referer" ([OpenWPM](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~arvindn/publications/OpenWPM_1_million_site_tracking_measurement.pdf)). For link decoration specifically, OAuth `state`/`code`, affiliate publisher IDs, search terms and SKUs are not user IDs. `utm_*` are campaign labels.

Encoding is the main source of false negatives. Chen et al. and CookieGraph check base64, MD5, SHA-1 and SHA-256 of the whole value and of its substrings, but CookieGraph still missed Pinterest's double-base64 `_pin_unauth` ([Chen et al.](https://spt.cut.ac.cy/papers/chen_www21.pdf); [Munir et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.12370)). Fouad et al. skipped POST bodies and so missed GA payloads ([Fouad et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01514)). Clype already records `postData`, which puts it ahead of that study.

## Since 5 Feb 2026, UK law exempts none of these findings

The amended PECR reg. 6(1) bans storing or accessing information on a user's device "subject to Schedule A1". Section 112 and Schedule 12 of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 have been fully in force since **5 Feb 2026 (SI 2026/82)** ([DUAA s.112](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/section/112); [DUAA Sch. 12](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/schedule/12)). The new statistics exception (Sch. A1 para 5) needs all of the following: the **sole purpose** is statistics to improve the service, the information is **not shared** except to help with that, clear information is given, and the user "does not object". The ICO's final guidance closes the gaps that matter here. A third-party analytics provider "must be a processor, not a joint controller" and must not "link it with other information". **"You must get consent for any cross-site or cross-device tracking."** **"Online advertising purposes are not exempt from PECR's consent requirements and never have been"** ([ICO: What are the exceptions?](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-the-use-of-storage-and-access-technologies/what-are-the-exceptions/)). Clicking "Reject all" is reasonably read as an objection, so even plain `_ga` sent to Google Analytics after rejection fails para 5(1)(e). That reading is an inference; no regulator text says it. Maximum PECR fines reportedly rose to the higher of **£17.5m or 4% of global turnover**, also from 5 Feb 2026. That comes from secondary sources ([Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/02/09/uk-data-reform-becomes-reality-what-this-means-for-you-and-what-you-need-to-do-n-102mh5x); [Bratby Law](https://bratby.law/duaa-takes-effect-ico-enforcement/)); the commencement SI was not confirmed. Conduct before 5 Feb 2026 falls under the old reg. 6, which had no analytics exemption.

EU sources are persuasive in the UK, not binding. They make the mechanism-level points the ICO has not spelled out. EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 v2 say tracking pixels "may link to an entity collecting many such requests and thus being able to track users' behaviour" (para 48). Tracking links append an identifier to the URL (para 49). Collecting such identifiers is "gaining of access" (para 51). Persistent identifiers "shared amongst several controllers to uniquely identify a person over different datasets", when collected by client-side code, engage Art. 5(3) (paras 61, 63) ([EDPB 2/2023](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2024-10/edpb_guidelines_202302_technical_scope_art_53_eprivacydirective_v2_en_0.pdf)). CNIL says CNAME cloaking "n'est pas illégale" (is not illegal) in itself, but warns that third parties can read authentication cookies ([CNIL](https://cnil.fr/fr/definition/cname-cloaking-ou-delegation-de-sous-domaine)). Frame CNAME findings as **transparency and security** issues, not as a banned technique. On personal data, a cookie ID that singles out a browser across sites is an "online identifier" under UK GDPR Art. 4(1) and Recital 30. CJEU *EDPS v SRB* (C-413/23 P, Sept 2025) makes the test relative to the holder, but that helps only recipients with no reasonable means to re-identify ([Bird & Bird](https://cm.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/eu-the-srb-decision-a-new-era-for-personal-data-and-data-processing-agreements)). An ad-tech firm receiving the same ID from many sites is not in that position. *Fashion ID* (joint control over transmission by an embedded pixel) and *Breyer* were **not re-verified** in this research. Check them on curia.europa.eu before citing them in a letter.

One wording rule follows. The observed request is itself the regulated act (EDPB para 51), so Clype never needs to prove what the recipient did afterwards. Write "**sent** X to Y, which **allows** Y to link…". Use "appears to" and "is consistent with". Never write "proves" or "illegal", and always cite evidence IDs, timestamps and the request URL. The strongest case is an ad-tech recipient plus a post-reject event.

## Five techniques to build next, in ranked order

The ranking weighs evidence strength against build cost on Clype's existing data. Time estimates are my own guesses for a developer who already has the capture loop, not measured. Build T1 first: T2 to T4 reuse its ID index and tokeniser.

| Rank | Technique | Proves | Needs new capture? | Est. time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ID index + leak matcher (with two-context diff) | First-party exfiltration, ID sharing, syncing | Yes: one extra fresh context per site (landing page only) | 2–2.5 h |
| 2 | Sync endpoint signatures + Prebid eids | Cookie matching / identity-graph IDs, even when hashed | No | 45 min |
| 3 | Same-context linkage pass | **Same ID recognised on site A and site B** (actual proof) | Yes: separate pass over 2–3 sites in one context | 1–1.5 h |
| 4 | CNAME cloaking check | Disguised third party, cookies leaked to it | DNS lookups only | 45 min |
| 5 | Static link-decoration scan | UID smuggling capability | One `eval_on_selector_all` | 30 min |

### T1. Build an ID index, then search every third-party request for those IDs

**Candidates.** Take every cookie from `context.cookies()` and from `Set-Cookie` headers in both phases (pre-consent and post-reject), plus first-party localStorage. Split values with `re.split(r'[^A-Za-z0-9_\-.=]', unquote(v))`. Also split on `.` so the GA `Z.C` form is caught, and keep the joined `Z.C` pair as its own candidate. Keep a token if **8 ≤ len ≤ 100** ([OpenWPM](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~arvindn/publications/OpenWPM_1_million_site_tracking_measurement.pdf)) and it is not one of these: an epoch timestamp (`^1[5-9]\d{8}$`, `^1[5-9]\d{11}$`), an ISO date, a property ID (`^(G|UA|GTM|AW|DC)-[A-Z0-9-]+$`), a locale (`^[a-z]{2}([-_][A-Za-z]{2})?$`), a URL, or a value of a consent cookie (`euconsent-v2`, `addtl_consent`, `gdpr_consent`, `us_privacy`, `OptanonConsent`, `OptanonAlertBoxClosed`, `CookieConsent`, `didomi_token`). Drop any value that changes between page loads in context A.

**Two-context confirmation.** Open context B right after A, load the landing page only, and reject the banner the same way (otherwise the consent state differs). For each (cookie domain, name, sub-field) seen in both contexts, compare the values. Identical means a constant: drop it. A length difference over 25% ([Demir et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.01498)) or `SequenceMatcher(None, a, b).ratio() ≥ 0.66` after timestamp and LCS stripping ([Chen et al.](https://spt.cut.ac.cy/papers/chen_www21.pdf)) means drop. Anything else is `id_confidence = "confirmed"`. A candidate absent from B is `"likely"`. Use the expiry rule (>90 days) only as a "persistent" flag, not as a filter ([Fouad et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01514); [Randall et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.10188)).

**Matching.** For each candidate `v`, precompute `v`, base64 (standard and URL-safe, with and without padding), and MD5, SHA-1 and SHA-256 hex digests ([Chen et al.](https://spt.cut.ac.cy/papers/chen_www21.pdf); [Blacklight](https://themarkup.org/blacklight/2020/09/22/how-we-built-a-real-time-privacy-inspector)). Tokenise each request the same way. Percent-decode up to 3 times, then cover path segments, query, fragment, `Referer`, response `Location`, and `postData`: walk JSON leaves, use `parse_qsl` for form data, and split lines for text beacons. Base64-decode any token of 16 or more characters that matches `[A-Za-z0-9+/=_-]` and tokenise the result too. A **hit** is a token equal to a variant, in a request whose eTLD+1 differs from the cookie's owner domain *and* belongs to a different Tracker Radar `owner`. Apply OpenWPM's sender and receiver direction rules.

**Classification and wording.**

| Condition | Finding type | Wording |
|---|---|---|
| First-party cookie value (e.g. `_ga`, `_fbp`) at a third-party receiver | `id_exfiltration` | "Your first-party ID `_fbp=fb.1.…` was sent to facebook.com [after you clicked 'Reject all']." |
| Same as above, and the receiver is ad-tech per Tracker Radar `categories`, or the same request carries a cross-site capable cookie for the receiver | `id_exfiltration` (cross-site linkable) | "…in the same request as facebook.com's own cookie, which lets Meta connect this visit to your browser's activity on other sites." |
| A third-party cookie ID appears in a request to a different-owner third party | `cookie_sync` | "[A] passed its ID for your browser to [B]. This lets both firms join up what they know about this browser." |
| A site's own session ID leaks via Referer | `referer_leak` | "Your session ID on this site leaked to [B] through the page address." |

Legal citations: PECR reg. 6 and Sch. A1 para 5(1)(c) and (e) ([DUAA Sch. 12](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/schedule/12)); the ICO "consent for any cross-site tracking" and "advertising never exempt" statements ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-the-use-of-storage-and-access-technologies/what-are-the-exceptions/)); UK GDPR Art. 4(1), 6, 7(3), and Art. 13(1)(e) if the recipient is missing from the policy (Clype's existing compare step can join on this). The severity of a GA-only `_ga` → google-analytics.com finding is "may fall outside the statistics exemption because you objected". Do not call it a breach. Remember that OpenWPM calls this method an *upper bound*.

### T2. Match cookie-sync endpoints and Prebid eids directly

These rules catch syncs even when the ID itself is hashed or encrypted, which plain string matching misses ([Papadopoulos et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/1805.10505v3)). Flag any request to `cm.g.doubleclick.net/pixel` with `google_nid`, `google_cm`, `google_push` or `google_hm`, and any URL with `google_gid=` ([Google cookie guide](https://developers.google.com/authorized-buyers/rtb/cookie-guide)). Record `google_error=3` as "attempted match, no Google cookie", which is useful context. Follow `request.redirected_from` chains so both hops of a sync (A → 302 → B?uid=… → 302 → A?partner=B) are reported. For Prebid, parse JSON `postData` and flag any non-empty `user.ext.eids` or `userIdAsEids`, listing each `source` (id5-sync.com, uidapi.com, liveramp.com, pubcid.org, criteo.com…) ([Prebid userId](https://docs.prebid.org/dev-docs/modules/userId.html)). Generic path patterns (`/usersync`, `/setuid`, `/getuid`, `/cksync`, `/match`) and vendor hosts such as `match.adsrvr.org` and `ib.adnxs.com/getuid` are **unverified general knowledge**. Use them only to raise confidence on a T1 hit, never alone. Wording: "After you clicked 'Reject all', the page ran an ad-tech 'cookie match' between [A] and [B] (evidence #…). Prebid's own documentation says these syncs should not run when cookies are denied." ([Prebid setConfig](https://docs.prebid.org/dev-docs/publisher-api-reference/setConfig.html)). Also cite EDPB 2/2023 paras 61 and 63 for identifiers shared among controllers.

### T3. Run a same-context linkage pass to prove cross-site recognition

This is the only technique that turns presence into proof, and it must not pollute the per-site audits. Keep the fresh-context-per-site audit as the baseline. Then run a separate **linkage pass**: one context visits the audited site, then 1–2 fixed reference sites known to embed common ad-tech (or two audited sites), rejecting each banner. For every third-party request, read the `Cookie` header via `request.all_headers()`. A **proven linkage** is the same cookie value, from a cookie that is cross-site capable (`sameSite=="None"`, secure, no `partitionKey`), sent to the same third-party owner while the top-level site was A and again while it was B. Tag each sighting with the top-level site and that site's consent phase. To show the value is an ID and not a constant, check it against T1's context-B value: it must differ there ([Englehardt & Narayanan](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~arvindn/publications/OpenWPM_1_million_site_tracking_measurement.pdf); local test in this research, consistent with [playwright#30384](https://patch-diff.githubusercontent.com/raw/microsoft/playwright/pull/30384.diff)). Also count a T1 token that reaches the same receiver from both sites. **Exclude** third-party localStorage or IndexedDB matches, because Playwright disables storage partitioning ([playwright#32230](https://github.com/microsoft/playwright/issues/32230)), and exclude any cookie with a `partitionKey`.

Wording: "The same identifier (`IDE=…`) was sent to doubleclick.net while this browser was on [site A] and again on [site B]. Google can therefore recognise it as the same browser across both sites. This happened after 'Reject all' was clicked on [site A]." Citations: ICO "You must get consent for any cross-site or cross-device tracking" ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-the-use-of-storage-and-access-technologies/what-are-the-exceptions/)); EDPB 2/2023 para 48; UK GDPR Art. 4(1) and 6. The existing cross-audit company view can then be upgraded with Privacy Badger's rule: a third party setting *ID-classified* cookies on **3 or more** audited sites ([Privacy Badger constants.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/EFForg/privacybadger/master/src/js/constants.js)). Label that "embedded widely", not "linked".

### T4. Resolve CNAMEs for same-site subdomains

For each request host with the same registrable domain as the site but a different host from the page, follow its CNAME chain with dnspython (`dns.resolver.resolve(h, "CNAME")`, iterated, max 10 hops). Flag the host when any chain member's eTLD+1 differs from the site's *and* suffix-matches AdGuard's `combined_original_trackers.txt` ([AdGuard](https://github.com/AdguardTeam/cname-trackers)). Only list matches count: CDNs and SaaS hosting are normal CNAME targets ([Dimova et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.09301)). The hardcoded suffix list in the notes (omtrdc.net, eulerian.net, at-o.net, wt-eu02.net…) is **unverified**. Use AdGuard's file instead. Then list which cookies in that request's `Cookie` header were **not set by the cloaked host**. Use Dimova's exclusions: the tracker's own cookies, session cookies, short values. Name any auth or session cookie explicitly. Record the resolver used, since answers vary. Wording: "`metrics.site.co.uk` is a DNS alias for [Adobe]. Requests that look first-party actually go to Adobe, and carried your `_ga` and `session_id` cookies." Cite UK GDPR Art. 5(1)(a) and 13(1)(e) for transparency, and Art. 32 if an auth cookie leaks; supporting sources are [CNIL](https://cnil.fr/fr/definition/cname-cloaking-ou-delegation-de-sous-domaine) and Dimova's vendor vulnerabilities. Never call the technique illegal in itself. This finding is cross-site only if T1 also finds the same ID at the vendor's own third-party domain. Server-side GTM on A records cannot be detected from DNS alone, so leave it out.

### T5. Scan outbound links for decoration, statically

After rejecting, run `page.eval_on_selector_all('a[href]', 'els => els.map(e => e.href)')` on each browsed page. Flag any href to a different eTLD+1 that carries a T1 ID value, or that goes through a known redirector (adclick.g.doubleclick.net, googleads.g.doubleclick.net, ad.doubleclick.net, gm.demdex.net, d.agkn.com, from [Randall et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.10188)) with an embedded destination URL. Click-ID parameter names (`gclid`, `fbclid`, `msclkid`, `_gl`, `mc_eid`, `ttclid`, `li_fat_id`…) are **unverified general knowledge**. Use them as a secondary label, and never flag `utm_*`. Don't click ads. Static inspection stays within the "passive only" rule. Also skip dynamic bounce tracking: GA's `_gl` linker decorates links on mousedown, so static scans under-report, and the report should say so. Wording: "Links on this page add your tracking ID (`_gl=…`) to the address of [other site], so the ID travels with you when you click." Cite EDPB 2/2023 paras 49–51 ([EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2024-10/edpb_guidelines_202302_technical_scope_art_53_eprivacydirective_v2_en_0.pdf)) and PECR reg. 6, which the ICO reads as technology-neutral. **No ICO text specifically on link decoration or CNAME was found.**

**Audit JSON note:** T1 to T5 add new finding types (`id_exfiltration`, `cookie_sync`, `cross_site_linkage`, `cname_cloaking`, `link_decoration`) and an `id_confidence` field. Under the project rules, record this as a change to the audit-format contract. Each finding should carry the evidence request IDs, consent phase, timestamp, and the matched encoding (`raw`, `b64`, `sha256`…).

## Conclusion

The shift that matters is from "who is present" to "which value moved where". A tracker that appears on every audited site but receives a different random ID each time is weaker evidence than one site's `_fbp` arriving at Meta beside Meta's own cookie. Clype's fresh-context design, which makes per-site baselines clean, is also the reason it currently cannot prove linking. The fix is not to give up isolation but to add two cheap extra observations: a second fresh context to confirm that values are IDs, and a deliberate shared context to show they are reused across sites. Both are standard methods from the literature, scaled down to a few minutes per site.

Three uncertainties should shape how hard Clype pushes its claims. No rule-based ID heuristic has published precision figures. Playwright's disabled storage partitioning means some apparent cross-site signals would not exist for real Chrome users. And server-side syncing, which Bashir et al. showed escapes browser heuristics, means a clean Clype report is never proof of no tracking. Since the 2026 PECR amendments, the legal test turns on the *sending*, and the ICO bars any exemption for cross-site tracking. A carefully scoped "sent X to Y after you rejected" is therefore both the most defensible wording and the most damaging.
